# Quark Babies --- Project Context

> **Purpose of this file:** A single source of truth for the Quark
> Babies team and anyone helping design, research, or build the NASA
> Space Apps Challenge project. It captures the challenge, the agreed
> concept, the selected missions and objects, the intended experience,
> technical direction, scope limits, and open decisions.
>
> **Status:** Working project brief. The concept and shortlist are
> agreed as the current direction, but implementation details and
> factual research still need validation against NASA primary sources.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. Project identity

-   **Team name:** Quark Babies
-   **Competition:** NASA International Space Apps Challenge 2026
-   **Selected challenge:** **"Abandoned but not Forgotten: Storytelling
    about NASA's Discarded Equipment on the Moon and Mars"**
-   **Core concept:** An immersive, interactive journey from Earth to
    the Moon and then Mars, revealing the history and legacy of selected
    equipment left behind by human space exploration.
-   **Central message:** **"Every mission leaves something behind. But
    not everything left behind is forgotten."**

The project should feel like an interactive space-history experience,
not a conventional information website or a catalogue of abandoned
objects.

## 2. Understanding the challenge

The selected challenge asks participants to tell stories about equipment
left behind during space exploration, particularly on the Moon and Mars.
The challenge description refers to equipment such as scientific
instruments, experiments, rovers, landers, probes, cameras, and other
exploration hardware.

The challenge's background emphasizes that: - Space missions have left
equipment on the Moon and Mars. - Some equipment completed its intended
mission; some continued to provide scientific value; and some stopped
functioning. - These objects are evidence of human exploration,
engineering, scientific ambition, and discovery. - Their stories can
help audiences understand the history and continuing value of space
exploration.

The challenge's objectives emphasize: - Making the history and
scientific contribution of the equipment understandable. - Engaging
audiences, especially school-age space enthusiasts. - Communicating
accurate information through an accessible and compelling experience. -
Using a creative format such as a website, interactive digital
experience, animation, documentary, or another suitable medium.

The challenge also encourages teams to consider: - What story they want
to tell. - How the story will be presented. - Who the intended audience
is. - What NASA data or resources will support the project. - What
perspective or format will make the story distinctive.

**Interpretation for Quark Babies:** The equipment and its
historical/scientific legacy must remain the heart of the project. The
cinematic journey and 3D interactions are the means of telling those
stories; they must not overshadow them.

## 3. The agreed creative direction

### A journey through the history of exploration

The experience is structured as a journey:

1.  **Earth --- The beginning:** Introduce a historic mission and its
    launch.
2.  **Moon --- Human exploration:** Explore selected Apollo-era
    equipment and its legacy.
3.  **Time-and-space transition:** Show that years have passed and that
    exploration technology and mission design have evolved.
4.  **Mars --- Robotic exploration:** Explore selected Mars missions and
    their equipment.
5.  **The legacy:** Bring the stories together and explain what these
    machines taught humanity.

This is a narrative transition between separate missions and eras. It is
**not** a claim that the Apollo 11 spacecraft travelled from the Moon to
Mars. The experience must clearly introduce a different spacecraft and
mission when it moves to Mars.

### The central narrative

The project should help visitors understand: - What an object is. - Why
NASA sent it. - What it did during the mission. - Where it was deployed
or left, when that is known. - What happened to it afterwards. - Whether
it is still operating or scientifically useful. - Why its story matters
today.

A visitor should be able to understand the significance of an object
without needing an advanced science or engineering background.

## 4. Current mission and object shortlist

The current agreed scope is **four missions and seven featured
objects**. This is deliberately selective. The goal is to tell a few
complete, memorable stories rather than display every mission or every
piece of equipment.

### Moon: two Apollo missions

#### Mission 1 --- Apollo 11 (1969)

Apollo 11 is the historic first crewed lunar landing and provides the
opening human-exploration chapter.

**Object 1: Lunar Module Descent Stage** - The lower descent portion of
the Eagle Lunar Module remained on the lunar surface after the
astronauts departed in the ascent stage. - Story focus: landing on the
Moon, the engineering needed to reach the surface, and the physical
remains of the first landing. - Visual potential: a recognizable lunar
module descent stage with landing legs, engine bell, and structural
details.

**Object 2: Lunar Laser Ranging Retroreflector** - A reflector array
deployed on the Moon that allows laser-ranging measurements from
Earth. - Story focus: an object left on the Moon that has continued to
support scientific measurements. - Visual potential: a compact
instrument with a distinctive array of corner-cube reflectors; suitable
for a close-up 3D inspection.

**Object 3: Apollo 11 Passive Seismic Experiment** - A scientific
experiment package deployed on the lunar surface to detect moonquakes
and other seismic activity. - Story focus: how equipment left behind
helped scientists investigate the Moon's interior and seismic
activity. - Visual potential: a compact instrument package with visible
structure and scientific purpose.

#### Mission 2 --- Apollo 15 (1971)

Apollo 15 represents a later stage of lunar exploration, when astronauts
could travel farther across the surface.

**Object 4: Lunar Roving Vehicle (LRV)** - The electric lunar vehicle
used by Apollo 15 astronauts to travel across the lunar surface; it was
left on the Moon. - Story focus: how the rover expanded the astronauts'
range and changed the way the Moon could be explored. - Visual
potential: one of the strongest 3D objects in the project because of its
wheels, chassis, seats, antenna, and equipment.

### Mars: two robotic missions

#### Mission 3 --- Mars Pathfinder (1997)

Pathfinder provides the transition from crewed lunar exploration to
robotic exploration on Mars.

**Object 5: Mars Pathfinder Lander** - The lander delivered the
Sojourner rover to the Martian surface and remained there. - Story
focus: the lander, its role in the mission, and the engineering behind
the landing system. - Visual potential: lander structure and deployed
ramps; the landing system can be explained with a short animation.

**Object 6: Sojourner Rover** - The small rover deployed by
Pathfinder. - Story focus: an early milestone in mobile robotic
exploration of Mars and what a small rover could accomplish. - Visual
potential: a compact rover with wheels, camera mast, and instrument
details.

#### Mission 4 --- Mars Exploration Rover: Opportunity (2004--2019)

Opportunity provides a compelling story about a robotic mission that
operated far beyond its original planned duration.

**Object 7: Opportunity Rover** - A Mars rover designed for a much
shorter primary mission but which operated for almost 15 years. - Story
focus: discoveries, its extended journey, and the dust-storm conditions
associated with the end of communications. - Visual potential: a
recognizable six-wheeled rover with a camera mast, solar panels, robotic
arm, and scientific instruments.

### Why these four missions?

-   **Apollo 11:** historic significance and the first human lunar
    landing.
-   **Apollo 15:** demonstrates how lunar exploration evolved and gives
    the project a strong, visually distinctive rover.
-   **Mars Pathfinder:** a milestone in robotic Mars exploration and a
    natural bridge from the Moon to Mars.
-   **Opportunity:** a memorable long-duration mission with a strong
    human-interest and scientific legacy story.

### What is intentionally excluded from the initial scope?

Do not add these as full interactive chapters unless the team
deliberately revises the scope: - Every other Apollo mission. - Spirit
rover. - Viking landers. - Curiosity and Perseverance as featured
abandoned objects. - Voyager and other deep-space missions. - Every
individual component inside each selected object. - Every Saturn V
rocket stage as a separate featured object.

Other missions can be mentioned briefly for context, but the initial
experience should remain focused on the seven selected objects.

## 5. Planned user experience and scene flow

### Scene 1 --- Earth / newspaper-style introduction

The first idea is a newspaper-inspired opening, possibly styled around
the Apollo 11 era.

Possible content: - A prominent historical headline about humanity's
first Moon landing. - Apollo 11 mission name. - Launch date: July 16,
1969. - Launch location and crew details. - A concise historical
introduction. - A clear action to begin the journey.

The newspaper is a creative starting point, not a final design decision.
The team may explore other opening treatments, but the opening should
establish the historical context before the launch sequence.

### Scene 2 --- Saturn V launch

-   Show the Apollo 11 Saturn V launch.
-   Include a countdown and launch date.
-   Animate the vehicle ascending from Earth.
-   Show stage separation in a scientifically responsible way.
-   Allow the visitor to understand that launch vehicle stages separate
    during ascent.

**Important factual distinction:** Saturn V stages discarded during
launch are not the same as equipment deliberately left on the Moon. Do
not present all separated rocket stages as lunar-surface objects. If the
launch sequence lets visitors inspect a separated stage, clearly explain
its actual fate and distinguish it from the seven featured objects.

### Scene 3 --- Journey to the Moon

-   Transition from Earth's atmosphere into space.
-   Introduce the Apollo spacecraft and the lunar journey.
-   Use narration and restrained ambient sound.
-   Establish the mission date and chronology.

The sequence should not imply that every part of the launch vehicle
remains attached throughout the journey.

### Scene 4 --- Lunar environment and object exploration

-   Arrive at a 3D lunar environment.
-   Show the Apollo 11 landing site and, when the experience moves to
    Apollo 15, the relevant Apollo 15 landing area.
-   Place markers for selected objects where reliable location data
    exists.
-   Let visitors select an object marker.
-   Move the camera towards the selected object.
-   Present the object's story and scientific/historical significance.

Potential object interactions: - Rotate and zoom the model. - View the
object in its lunar context. - Trigger an exploded-view animation to
reveal major components. - Select a component to see its function. -
Read or listen to a concise explanation. - View mission date, location,
and status.

**Exploded-view guidance:** The animation should separate components in
a controlled engineering-style presentation. It should not look like the
object is being destroyed. Component-level detail should be included
only where accurate model geometry and reliable information are
available.

### Scene 5 --- Time-and-space transition

After the lunar chapters: - Pull the camera away from the lunar
surface. - Show the Moon receding into the distance. - Transition
through a starfield or other cinematic space environment. - Communicate
the passage of time with a visible year or timeline. - Change the
spacecraft visual to represent a different era and mission. - Introduce
Mars and robotic exploration.

The narration should explain that this is a transition between different
missions and generations of exploration technology, not one continuous
Apollo spacecraft journey.

### Scene 6 --- Mars environment and object exploration

-   Arrive at a 3D Martian landscape.
-   Introduce Mars Pathfinder and Sojourner.
-   Later introduce Opportunity as a separate mission and time period.
-   Place location markers where reliable location data exists.
-   Reuse the core object-exploration interaction pattern from the Moon.
-   Adapt the environment, terrain, object models, and narration to
    Mars.

### Scene 7 --- Legacy / closing

End by connecting the stories: - What did these machines help humanity
discover? - Which objects continued to provide scientific value? - What
did their missions teach future engineers and scientists? - Why should
people remember equipment that was left behind?

The closing should reinforce the project's central message: **Every
mission leaves something behind. But not everything left behind is
forgotten.**

## 6. Audio and storytelling direction

Audio is an important part of the intended experience.

Use three distinct audio layers:

1.  **Narration:** Explains the historical events, mission context,
    equipment purpose, discoveries, and legacy.
2.  **Ambient sound:** Creates atmosphere for Earth, launch, space, the
    Moon, and Mars.
3.  **Interaction sound:** Provides subtle feedback when visitors select
    an object, open information, or trigger a transition.

Audio requirements: - Provide a mute control. - Provide volume
control. - Allow narration to be replayed. - Include captions or
subtitles. - Avoid forcing visitors to listen to continuous narration. -
Keep narration concise and synchronized with the scene.

Scientific accuracy: - Space is a vacuum. Do not present cinematic
external rocket or explosion sounds as literal sounds heard by an
observer outside a spacecraft. - Cinematic sound design is acceptable
when treated as atmosphere rather than a scientific recording. - Do not
invent mission dialogue, quotations, or recordings.

## 7. Scientific accuracy and research rules

Scientific and historical accuracy is a core requirement, not a
finishing touch.

### Object status categories

Where relevant, distinguish among: - Returned to Earth. - Discarded
during launch or flight. - Left in lunar orbit. - Left on the lunar
surface. - Left on the Martian surface. - Still operational. - No longer
operational. - Status or precise location uncertain.

Do not label every object "abandoned" in the same sense. Some equipment
was deliberately left behind; some became inactive; some continued to
operate or provide data.

### Coordinates and locations

-   Use real coordinates only when supported by a reliable source.
-   Record the source and coordinate reference for every displayed
    location.
-   Distinguish precise coordinates from approximate locations.
-   If an object's location is unknown or uncertain, say so instead of
    inventing a marker.
-   Do not imply that every piece of debris or every component has a
    known exact location.

### 3D models and imagery

-   Prefer authentic NASA models, images, diagrams, and mission material
    where available and appropriate.
-   Check the provenance and usage terms of every external asset.
-   If a model is simplified or recreated, label it as an illustrative
    reconstruction.
-   Do not imply that an illustrative model is an exact engineering
    replica.
-   Ensure the displayed object matches the correct mission and
    configuration.

### Research record

For each featured object, maintain a small research record containing: -
Official object and mission name. - Mission date and relevant event
dates. - Purpose and scientific/engineering role. - Deployment or final
known location. - What happened to it. - Operational or current status,
if known. - Important discoveries or contribution. - Source URLs and
access notes. - Confidence or uncertainty notes. - 3D model/image source
and asset terms.

Use NASA primary sources wherever possible, such as mission pages,
mission reports, technical documents, image archives, and NASA data
portals.

### Technical principles

-   Build a small working experience before attempting every cinematic
    feature.
-   Optimize models, textures, audio, and terrain for ordinary laptops
    and mobile devices where feasible.
-   Provide a fallback if a 3D model or heavy scene cannot load.
-   Avoid loading every high-resolution asset at the start.
-   Keep mission facts and object data separate from the 3D presentation
    code.
-   Make navigation and object information usable even if the visitor
    disables audio.
-   Test performance early; do not leave optimization until the end.

## 9. Scope and prioritization

The project can become too large if every idea is attempted at full
fidelity. The initial target should be a complete, polished, working
experience with a limited number of well-researched objects.

### Must-have

-   A clear Earth → Moon → Mars narrative.
-   A working opening and launch sequence.
-   A clear distinction between launch-stage separation and equipment
    left on planetary surfaces.
-   A navigable lunar and Martian experience.
-   All seven selected objects represented in the experience, with at
    least basic accurate information.
-   Interactive object selection and readable object stories.
-   A time-and-space transition that clearly introduces a new
    mission/era.
-   At least one NASA data/resource source used meaningfully.
-   Functional navigation, captions, and audio controls.
-   A presentable, working final project.

### Should-have

-   Exploded-view interaction for selected objects.
-   Verified coordinate markers.
-   Object status indicators.
-   Mission timeline.
-   Narration and ambient sound.
-   Authentic NASA 3D models or imagery.
-   Component-level information for objects where reliable details are
    available.

### Stretch goals

-   Highly detailed planetary terrain.
-   Fully cinematic launch and landing sequences.
-   Detailed component-level disassembly for all seven objects.
-   Advanced lighting and atmospheric effects.
-   Multiple narration paths.
-   Additional missions or objects.

**Scope rule:** If time is limited, reduce visual complexity or the
number of detailed component interactions before sacrificing factual
accuracy, object stories, or a complete working journey.

## 10. Audience and design principles

The challenge specifically mentions engaging school-age space
enthusiasts and making the subject understandable to wider audiences.

Design for a curious visitor who may know very little about space
history.

The experience should: - Explain technical terms in plain language. -
Lead with the story and then reveal technical detail. - Use short, clear
information sections. - Make it obvious what can be clicked or
explored. - Provide context before showing complex models. - Avoid
overwhelming the visitor with statistics. - Use visual storytelling
without sacrificing factual accuracy. - Work for visitors who do not use
sound.

A useful information hierarchy for every object:

1.  **The story:** What is this, and why should I care?
2.  **The mission:** Why did NASA send it?
3.  **The engineering:** What did it do and how did it work?
4.  **The fate:** What happened to it?
5.  **The legacy:** What did it teach us?

Anyone helping Quark Babies should follow these rules:

    four missions and seven featured objects.
2.  **Do not invent scientific facts, coordinates, equipment status, or

    possible.**

    unfinished one.**

    audio honestly.**

## 14. One-paragraph project summary

**Quark Babies is developing an immersive interactive storytelling
experience for NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026's "Abandoned but not
Forgotten" challenge. The experience takes visitors on a narrative
journey from Earth to the Moon and then Mars, exploring four landmark
missions---Apollo 11, Apollo 15, Mars Pathfinder, and Mars Exploration
Rover: Opportunity---through seven carefully selected pieces of
equipment. Visitors will encounter mission history, 3D environments,
location-based object discovery, equipment explanations, and selected
exploded-view interactions, supported by narration and atmospheric
sound. The project aims to show that equipment left behind is more than
discarded hardware: it is evidence of human ingenuity and a continuing
legacy of scientific exploration.**
